# Source Evaluations

## A. G. Dickens — Source Evaluation

**Provenance:**
Dickens was a lifelong academic historian who published The English Reformation in 1964 while at King's College London, at the height of his career. He later became Director of the Institute of Historical Research, giving him extraordinary influence over the field. A revised second edition appeared in 1989 in response to revisionist criticism.

**Purpose:**
Dickens set out to write a comprehensive, authoritative national survey of the English Reformation — the first genuinely scholarly one since the nineteenth century. His purpose was to demonstrate that the Reformation was a genuine popular movement rooted in pre-existing religious discontent, not merely a top-down political imposition. He wanted to rehabilitate Protestant ideas as a serious causal force, against the then-prevailing view that Henry's political decisions were all that mattered.

**Context:**
Written in the early 1960s, at a time when post-war Britain was still broadly Protestant in cultural self-understanding, and when academic historiography was dominated by political and constitutional history in the Elton mould. The revisionist challenge from Duffy, Haigh and Scarisbrick had not yet emerged; Dickens was writing into a largely sympathetic audience for the Protestant-triumph narrative. His experience of Lübeck at the war's end — a Hanseatic city with strong Reformation connections — may also have deepened his interest in the popular roots of Protestantism. By the 1980s his framework was under sustained attack and "Dickensianism" had become a term of historiographical abuse.

**Tone:**
Broad, confident, and declarative — the tone of a scholar staking out a comprehensive national narrative rather than arguing a narrow thesis. His language is readable and direct; the Telegraph obituary praises the book's accessibility. There is, however, an unmistakably Protestant sympathetic undertone: Catholicism is described through the lens of what it lacked rather than what it offered, and popular religious practice is read as superstition rather than genuine devotion.

**Content:**
Dickens argues that late medieval English Catholicism was corrupt and spiritually shallow, that Lollardy provided a "springboard of critical dissent" that Protestant ideas could build on, and that the Reformation succeeded because Protestant doctrine — sola fide, sola scriptura — genuinely captured the popular imagination, particularly in the "great crescent" of southeast England. His central claim is that Protestant ideas were spreading independently of Henry's political crisis and would eventually have produced reform regardless.

## Eamon Duffy — Source Evaluation

**Provenance:**
Duffy is Professor of the History of Christianity at Cambridge, a cradle Catholic layman and one of the most prominent Catholic voices in British public intellectual life. The Stripping of the Altars was published in 1992 and is widely regarded as the most influential work of English Reformation history of the late twentieth century. A second, revised edition appeared in 2005.

**Purpose:**
Duffy set out explicitly to rehabilitate pre-Reformation English Catholicism — to demonstrate that it was not the corrupt, superstition-ridden, spiritually exhausted institution Dickens had portrayed, but a rich, coherent, and overwhelmingly popular religious culture. His secondary purpose was to argue that the Reformation was therefore not a popular liberation but an imposed destruction — a "great cultural hiatus" forced on a contented population. He describes the book as "an elegy for a world we had lost."

**Context:**
Written against the backdrop of a field that had been dominated since 1964 by Dickens's Protestant-triumphalist narrative. Duffy was part of a broader revisionist movement in the 1980s-90s that included Scarisbrick and Haigh, all of whom were reacting against what they saw as the Whiggish assumptions built into Dickens's account. His work also coincided with a general turn in social and cultural history toward "history from below" — the study of ordinary people's religious lives rather than elite legislation. His Catholic identity made him unusually empathetic to the pre-Reformation world he was studying, though critics argue this crossed into advocacy.

**Tone:**
Elegiac, immersive, and deliberately counter-polemic. Duffy writes with evident personal feeling about the Catholic practices he describes; his tone is warm toward the pre-Reformation world and cool toward the reformers. The scale of the book — nearly 700 pages, spending 400 on pre-Reformation religion alone — enacts his argument structurally, giving far more space to what was lost than to what replaced it. Marshall describes it as having "swept away the old certainties as surely as the reformers swept away the altars."

**Content:**
Duffy argues that pre-Reformation Catholicism was "resolutely Christocentric" rather than saint-obsessed; that popular religious practice was theologically coherent rather than superstitious; and that there was no significant popular demand for Protestant reform. He draws heavily on churchwardens' accounts, wills, and liturgical texts. He largely omits Lollardy in his first edition (dismissing it as "grossly exaggerated" in the second). His thesis on Mary's reign — that the Marian persecution was both inevitable and to a considerable extent successful — is his most controversial claim.

## Diarmaid MacCulloch — Source Evaluation

**Provenance:**
MacCulloch is Professor of the History of the Church at Oxford, an ordained Anglican deacon who declined full priesthood because of the Church's position on homosexuality, and describes himself as "a candid friend of Christianity." His key works include Tudor Church Militant (1999) and the panoramic Reformation: Europe's House Divided 1490-1700 (2003). He has also written the standard biography of Thomas Cranmer.

**Purpose:**
MacCulloch's purpose is synthetic and postrevisionist: to rescue the Reformation from both the Protestant triumphalism of Dickens and the Catholic revisionism of Duffy, by showing that both were regionally partial. He wants to demonstrate that the Reformation was neither a smooth popular success nor a pure top-down imposition, but a genuinely complex and regionally differentiated process that included real popular Protestant conviction in some areas alongside genuine Catholic resistance in others. He is also explicitly motivated by a desire to counter what he sees as the distorting influence of Catholic revisionism on the field.

**Context:**
Writing from the 1990s onward, MacCulloch works in the fully postrevisionist phase of the historiography, after both the Dickensian and the revisionist orthodoxies have been challenged. His personal context matters too: as a gay man who has experienced exclusion by the institution he studies, he is self-consciously alert to how insiders and outsiders experience religious institutions differently, and how the historical record tends to suppress outsider voices. His 2013 Spectator interview makes explicit the connection between his personal experience of marginalisation and his historical methodology.

**Tone:**
Scholarly but engaged, arguing against several contemporaries simultaneously. His tone is more combative than Duffy's — he explicitly accuses Duffy of being "a Catholic historian rather than a historian who is Catholic" — and more synthesising than Haigh's. He is willing to acknowledge uncertainty and provisional conclusions ("the most difficult to substantiate") in a way that makes his arguments more methodologically honest than either Dickens or Duffy.

**Content:**
MacCulloch argues for "as many Reformations as there were monarchs on the Tudor throne after the break with Rome," plus additional reformations at the popular and clerical level. He revives Dickens's Lollard argument but qualifies it regionally, identifying a "striking coincidence" between former Lollard strongholds and early popular evangelicalism, and documenting transmission links through the "Christian Brethren" network. He emphasises the influence of Zurich and Heinrich Bullinger on English Protestant theology, and the role of the printing press in spreading ideas. He treats Edward VI's reign as a decisive Protestant moment that the older historiography underplays.

## G. R. Elton — Source Evaluation

**Provenance:**
Elton was born Gottfried Rudolph Ehrenberg in Germany in 1921; his Jewish family fled to England in 1939 and he anglicised his name. He became the dominant figure in Tudor political history from the 1950s until his death in 1994, holding a chair at Cambridge. His key work for this essay is Reform and Reformation: England, 1508-1558 (1977), though his wider thesis about Thomas Cromwell's "revolution in government" in the 1530s underpins his account of the Reformation throughout.

**Purpose:**
Elton's purpose was to demonstrate that the English Reformation was primarily a political event — the product of decisions made by the monarch and his chief minister, Thomas Cromwell — rather than a religious or popular one. He wanted to establish Cromwell as the decisive individual who created the modern English state, giving Parliament its sovereignty and the Crown its administrative machinery. His broader purpose was to insist on the primacy of political history and original documentary sources against what he saw as the encroachment of social history and theoretical models.

**Context:**
Writing in the 1950s-70s, Elton was working in a field still dominated by constitutional and political history; his agenda was simultaneously to counter Dickens's bottom-up Protestant narrative and to establish his own top-down political one. His background as a German Jewish refugee who had experienced totalitarianism at close range likely deepened his conviction that political decisions — made by individuals in positions of power — are the primary engines of historical change; he was instinctively resistant to impersonal structural explanations.

**Tone:**
Combative, authoritative, and often scathing. Elton was notorious for harsh criticism of other scholars and was "often scathing in his comments about the work of other scholars" and "willingly spoke his mind." His prose is direct and confident to the point of dogmatism. His eulogy notes that "behind the rhetorical fireworks lay a genuine concern for a discipline increasingly subject to the whims of ideologues."

**Content:**
Elton argues the Reformation was driven from the top — by Henry's need for a divorce and Cromwell's administrative genius — not by popular Protestant demand. His key 1977 claim is that by 1553 England was "almost certainly nearer to being a Protestant country than to anything else," suggesting that even in his power-focused account, Protestant ideas had genuinely succeeded by Edward's reign. He credits Cromwell with creating national sovereignty, parliamentary supremacy over the Church, and a reformed central administration — making the Reformation a constitutional and governmental revolution as much as a religious one.

## Christopher Haigh — Source Evaluation

**Provenance:**
Haigh is an Oxford historian who began with a detailed local study of Tudor Lancashire (1975) and developed from it a national revisionist argument. He edited the key revisionist volume The English Reformation Revised (1987) and published his own full survey, English Reformations (1993). Despite being regularly grouped with the "Catholic revisionists," he is in fact a self-described Anglican agnostic.

**Purpose:**
Haigh's purpose is to demolish the idea of a single, unified "English Reformation" — either Dickens's popular Protestant success story or Elton's top-down political revolution — and replace it with a picture of multiple, overlapping, discontinuous reformations that varied enormously by region, period, and social group. He wants to show that Protestant ideas were imposed on a largely unwilling or indifferent population rather than welcomed, and that the "success" of the Reformation is a retrospective illusion created by Protestant historians writing from inside the tradition they are studying.

**Context:**
Haigh's Lancashire study placed him squarely in a region where Catholicism was exceptionally strong and Protestantism exceptionally weak — a regional base that arguably skewed his national conclusions. He was writing in the 1970s-90s in a society becoming steadily less religious and more secular, which may have made him more alert than earlier historians to the possibility that most ordinary Tudor people were simply indifferent to theological questions rather than committed to either side. The classroom material also notes the parallel with Cold War historiography: just as the collapse of the USSR showed each Eastern European country had its own specific reasons for rejecting Communism, Haigh saw each local English Reformation as having its own specific causes rather than a single national story.

**Tone:**
Polemical and deliberately provocative. His titles are chosen to signal dissent — English Reformations rather than The English Reformation; "Success and Failure in the English Reformation" rather than Dickens's triumphant narrative. His closing verdict in English Reformations — that by 1590 "that was how it had been in 1530" — is a deliberately deflating rejection of the whole premise that anything fundamental had changed. He is described as "lively and readable," with a talent for the memorable formulation.

**Content:**
Haigh argues for plural, discontinuous reformations driven by political contingency rather than religious ideas. His key claim is that Protestant conversion at the popular level was slow, shallow, and largely confined to southeast England; his evidence from visitation reports (borrowed via Gerald Strauss's framework for Germany) suggests that even by 1600 most English people knew their catechism only by rote, without having internalised Protestant doctrine. His dismissal of any grand underlying cause makes him the historian most directly hostile to the essay statement's premise.

## J. J. Scarisbrick — Source Evaluation

**Provenance:**
Scarisbrick is a Catholic academic historian who held a chair at the University of Warwick. He published Henry VIII in 1968 and The Reformation and the English People in 1984. He is also co-founder of the pro-life charity LIFE, established in 1970. His Reformation and the English People covers only the period 1536-1553, a significant limitation in scope.

**Purpose:**
In Henry VIII, Scarisbrick's purpose was to challenge Elton's thesis by restoring Henry himself — rather than Cromwell — to the centre of the Reformation story, arguing Henry was committed to ecclesiastical reform before Cromwell arrived on the scene. In The Reformation and the English People, his purpose shifted toward revisionism: demonstrating that ordinary English men and women did not want the Reformation and were slow to accept it when it came. Both books serve the implicit purpose of rehabilitating a Catholic reading of the period against the Protestant-nationalist historiography that had dominated since the nineteenth century.

**Context:**
Scarisbrick was writing Henry VIII at roughly the same time as Elton was at the height of his influence, and his challenge to Elton's "revolution in government" thesis created a direct scholarly confrontation between two historians with very different frameworks. By the time of The Reformation and the English People (1984), Scarisbrick was part of a broader revisionist wave that included Duffy and Haigh. He is explicitly aware of the dangers of overcorrection, conceding in his Catholic Herald interview that "we are in danger of making the Reformation inexplicable" if revisionism goes too far.

**Tone:**
Scholarly and relatively measured, though with an unmistakable Catholic sympathetic undertone. He describes himself as "a rather unsubtle sort of chap" — a self-deprecating framing that somewhat masks the genuine sophistication of his dual contribution as both a Top-Down and a Revisionist historian. His key sentence — "on the whole, English men and women did not want the Reformation and most of them were slow to accept it when it came" — is the cleanest single-sentence statement of the revisionist position and was designed for maximum impact.

**Content:**
In Henry VIII, Scarisbrick argues that Henry himself — not Cromwell — was the driving force behind the break with Rome, and that Cromwell's administrative "revolution" was neither revolutionary nor original. In The Reformation and the English People, he draws on parish-level sources (churchwardens' accounts, wills, guild records) to argue that popular attachment to Catholic religious practice was deep and genuine, that there was minimal popular demand for Protestant reform, and that the Reformation was experienced by most ordinary people as an unwanted imposition rather than a welcome liberation. His book's chronological limitation to 1536-1553 means he does not engage with Mary's reign or Elizabeth's settlement, which weakens his ability to explain what happened after Edward's death.
